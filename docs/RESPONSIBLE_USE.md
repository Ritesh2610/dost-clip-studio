# Use it responsibly

Dost Clip Studio makes it quick to cut short clips from a long video. That is useful for your own videos and for videos you have been allowed to clip. It is not a way around the rights of the people who made a video.

This page is general information written for people who use the tool. It is not legal advice. Copyright law differs between countries, and platform rules change. If a decision matters to you, read the platform's current rules and ask a lawyer.

## Permission comes first

A video belongs to the person or company that made it. Cutting a part out of it and posting that part somewhere is a use of their work. In general, what makes that lawful is permission from the owner.

- If you made the video, you can clip it.
- If someone else made it, ask them, and keep their answer. A message that says "yes, you can post clips of my streams on your channel" is far better than an assumption.
- Check what the permission covers. "You can clip my streams" may not include putting adverts on the clips, posting them on every platform or using them to promote something.
- Some creators publish clipping rules for their community. Follow them exactly, including any rule about credit, links or monetisation.

Things that do not count as permission:

- The video is public, or the link was easy to find.
- You credit the creator. Credit is good manners and often a condition of permission, but it does not replace permission. YouTube's own help pages say the same about giving credit to a copyright owner.
- The clip is short. No fixed number of seconds is automatically allowed.
- Other accounts are already posting clips of the same video.

Many countries have exceptions for uses such as criticism, review, news reporting or teaching, called fair use or fair dealing. They depend on the details of each case, they differ from country to country, and a platform does not have to agree with your reading of them. Do not rely on them without advice.

## One video can have several owners

Permission from the creator covers what the creator owns. A video often contains other people's work:

- **Games.** The game shown in a stream belongs to its publisher. Many publishers publish a video policy that says what creators may do with footage. Some limit monetisation or particular parts of a game. The streamer's permission does not change the publisher's rules.
- **Music.** A song playing in the background has its own owners, usually a label and a publisher. A clip can be claimed, muted or blocked because of its music even when the creator is happy for you to post it.
- **Other clips and broadcasts.** If the video shows a film, a match, a TV programme or another creator's video, that part belongs to someone else.
- **People.** Guests and people who appear in a video may have their own rights and expectations, especially in private settings.

When you choose a moment, listen for music and look at what is on screen. A clip of the creator talking is simpler than a clip with a chart song behind it.

## A platform can still remove or limit a clip

Having permission does not oblige a platform to show your clip to anyone. Platforms apply their own rules on top of the law, and they change them.

What follows is a summary, in our words, of what the platforms have published. It was checked in November 2026. Read the current pages before you rely on it.

### Instagram

In April 2024 Instagram announced changes to how it recommends reels ("Helping Creators Find New Audiences", on [creators.instagram.com](https://creators.instagram.com/recommendations-and-originality)). It said that:

- When it finds identical copies of a piece of content, it recommends only the original. In recommendations, the original takes the place of the repost.
- It adds a label to reposted content that links to the original creator.
- Content that has been changed in a significant way is not replaced. Its examples include a new voiceover and a remix that shows a reaction.
- Accounts that repeatedly post other people's content without creating it or improving it in a material way are treated as aggregators and left out of recommendations. People who follow those accounts still see their posts.
- To be recommended at all, content must have no visible watermark.

In April 2026 Instagram extended this to photo and carousel posts and published its [original content guidelines](https://creators.instagram.com/original-content-guidelines/). According to that page:

- Original content is work you made yourself, or other people's material that you edited in a material way.
- Adding a border or a watermark, changing the speed, changing only the post's caption or simply crediting the original creator are minor edits. They do not make a repost original.
- Material edits add something of yours: narration, a storyline, or text and graphics that add context instead of only describing what is on screen.
- An account that mostly posts unoriginal content may not be recommended to people who do not follow it. It becomes eligible again when most of what it posted over a 30-day period is original.
- These guidelines are separate from Instagram's rules on intellectual property. Getting permission is still up to you, and permission does not make a repost count as original.

What this means for clips: a plain cut of someone else's video, posted from your account, may not be recommended to anyone who does not already follow you, and the original may be shown instead. The crop and the captions this app adds are unlikely to change that on their own. Your own commentary or context might.

### YouTube

YouTube's rules come from two separate places.

**Copyright.** An owner can ask YouTube to remove a video that uses their work, and YouTube's Content ID system can claim, block or mute a video automatically when it matches a song or a broadcast. Removal requests lead to copyright strikes, and YouTube says that repeated strikes can end a channel.

**Monetisation.** YouTube's "channel monetization policies" page ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)) has a rule on reused content. In short:

- A channel that reposts material from elsewhere without adding significant original commentary, changes, or educational or entertainment value cannot earn money through the YouTube Partner Program.
- Its examples of what is not allowed include clips of a show edited together with little or no narrative, and content that many other creators have already uploaded.
- YouTube says this rule is separate from copyright. It can apply even when you have permission from the original creator and no copyright claim has been made.
- It is applied to the channel as a whole, not to single videos.

YouTube's Shorts monetisation policies also say that views of non-original Shorts, such as reuploads of another creator's content with nothing original added, do not count towards Shorts revenue.

What this means for clips: with permission you may be able to post a clip, but a channel made only of unchanged clips of other people's videos is unlikely to be accepted for monetisation.

## What the tool does to help

The app cannot know whether you have permission. It does a few things to keep honest use easy and careless use harder:

- **A permission checkbox that the server enforces.** Before any analysis you must tick "I made this video, or I have permission from its owner to clip it." The server refuses an analysis request that does not carry that confirmation, so the check cannot be skipped by calling the app directly.
- **A list of allowed hosts.** By default the app accepts links only from YouTube (`youtube.com` and `youtu.be`). It is not a general downloader.
- **A credit line.** The post text the app suggests for each clip includes the line `Clip from <channel> on YouTube` when the source has a channel name. Keep it, and add a link to the original if the creator asks for one.
- **No posting, no bulk tools.** The app does not upload, post or schedule anything, and it has no feature for working through a list of channels or videos. It renders at most 10 clips at a time from one video that you chose, and you post each one yourself.
- **No promises.** The app shows which moments are strongest by its measurements. It does not claim a clip will be popular.

What the tool does not do: it does not check ownership, detect music, read a publisher's video policy or tell you whether a platform will accept a clip. Those checks are yours.

## A short checklist before you post

1. Is this my video, or do I have the owner's permission in writing?
2. Does the permission cover this platform and, if it applies, monetisation?
3. Is there music, game footage or someone else's video in the clip that needs its own permission?
4. Have I credited the creator in the way they asked?
5. Have I added something of my own, such as a title, context or commentary, where the platform expects it?

If the answer to the first question is no, do not make the clip.
